# Literature review: adjustment, lock-in, and the missing exit pathway

Research on sustainability transitions explains why decarbonization rarely unfolds as a simple replacement of one technology by another. Carbon lock-in arises from mutually reinforcing technological, institutional, and political arrangements (Unruh, 2000), while increasing returns and policy feedback can make initially reasonable policy trajectories difficult to reverse (Pierson, 2000). Transition scholarship consequently recognises multiple pathways of change rather than a single linear sequence (Geels & Schot, 2007; Markard et al., 2012). Environmental and financial policy can redirect innovation, investment, and operating practices (Acemoglu et al., 2012; Campiglio, 2016). These insights establish why lower coal intensity, tighter pollution control, and renewable expansion are meaningful transition outcomes. They do not, however, establish that the stock of incumbent coal assets has exited. A coal unit may run fewer hours, improve its heat rate, comply with tighter pollution standards, or be supplemented by renewable generation while remaining operational. This distinction motivates an analytical separation between operating adjustment, compositional change, and asset exit.

A growing coal-phaseout literature addresses the exit margin directly. Plant-level and scenario-based studies quantify the operational-lifetime constraint that coal fleets face under Paris-consistent pathways and identify potential retirement sequences for China (Cui et al., 2019, 2021). They also show why a Paris-consistent pathway need not mean immediate physical closure of every unit: capacity payments can retain part of the fleet as reserve capacity during renewable expansion (Yin et al., 2021), while hourly Chinese power-system modelling finds that repositioning coal from baseload to flexible capacity can reduce stranded-asset pressure and support renewable integration under a binding carbon constraint (An et al., 2025). Other work links early retirement to just-transition outcomes, identifies politically sensitive intervention points, and compares mandatory phaseout instruments with carbon-market policies (Zhang et al., 2022; Heerma van Voss et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023). This literature makes clear that coal exit requires more than a general preference for low-carbon investment: it requires decisions over retirement timing, allocation of debt and compensation, remediation, replacement services, and constraints on new high-carbon investment. Studies of stranded assets and transition feasibility further show that these decisions distribute costs across owners, lenders, workers, local governments, and consumers (Johnson et al., 2015; van der Ploeg & Venables, 2020; Muttitt et al., 2023). Yet most contributions ask which plants should retire in a normative or modelled pathway. Less attention has been paid to whether an observed period of cleaner operation, renewable deployment, and environmental-financial constraint has in fact generated accelerated retirement of incumbent coal assets.

The Chinese power-system literature supplies an essential qualification to any exit argument. Decarbonization depends on a portfolio of replacement and integration resources, including storage, transmission, hydro flexibility, dispatch reform, and renewable absorption (He et al., 2016, 2020; Liu et al., 2019). In a coal-heavy grid, dispatch and grid rules influence whether variable renewable generation substitutes for thermal output rather than merely adding capacity (Davidson et al., 2016), while reductions in curtailment reflect both system and policy change (Chen et al., 2022). China’s wind and solar potential is considerable (Wang et al., 2023), but a lower thermal generation share does not itself determine the disposition of a coal unit. Coal may continue to supply capacity, flexibility, or reliability services, and the cost of providing equivalent low-carbon services can be material (Gonzalez-Salazar et al., 2018; Zhuo et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2021; An et al., 2025). The relevant policy question is therefore not whether all coal units should close immediately, but whether a credible replacement package permits an orderly change in their role and eventual exit.

This distinction also clarifies the role of green finance and Chinese transition policy. China’s green-credit framework embeds environmental risk in lending practice and can influence lending conditions, retrofit investment, and renewable investment (Aizawa & Yang, 2010; Zhang et al., 2011; CBRC, 2012). It does not, by itself, specify a closure date for an existing generator, resolve owner debt, provide replacement capacity, or compensate coal-dependent localities. The broader policy mix similarly contains instruments directed at pollution control, power-market reform, renewable consumption, coal-unit upgrading, system security, and capacity remuneration. These policies constitute a substantial transition architecture, but their presence should not be conflated with a fleet-wide exit programme. Without bank-to-owner or bank-to-plant lending and debt data, an empirical analysis cannot attribute either operating adjustment or the absence of retirement acceleration to the Green Credit Guidelines alone.

Finally, regional-transition research shows why asset exit cannot be treated as a plant-level engineering decision. Coal dependence is embedded in local employment, fiscal revenue, state-owned assets, and industrial linkages, and these conditions vary markedly across regions (Hansen & Coenen, 2015; Newell & Mulvaney, 2013; Jiang et al., 2023). Comparative coal-transition evidence and the transitional-assistance literature therefore stress coordinated measures for affected workers, places, and institutions (Diluiso et al., 2021; Green & Gambhir, 2020). Building on these literatures, this study does not claim that China lacks energy-transition policy, nor does it treat post-2012 provincial variation as a clean green-credit experiment. It instead uses direct coal-unit histories alongside provincial operating, compositional, and constraint measures to diagnose whether adjustment spilled over into asset exit. The contribution is to show how real progress at the operating and composition margins can coexist with continued coal-asset accumulation, thereby revealing a policy-design problem: the absence of an integrated retirement, replacement, finance, and local-adjustment pathway.

## Evidence and writing boundary

- Scenario models and plant-level retirement rankings explain what a feasible exit pathway requires; they do not validate this study’s historical findings.
- Policy documents define instruments and institutional context. Exact claims on targets, clauses, or implementation require an archived primary source.
- Green credit remains institutional context and a boundary on research design; it should not be reframed as this study’s causal treatment.
